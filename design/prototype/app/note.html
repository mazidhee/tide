A replicated database keeps a copy of the same data on several machines, so that it survives the loss of one and can serve reads from more than one place. With a single leader, every write goes to the leader first. Followers apply the same changes, in the same order, from the leader’s replication log.

Synchronous or asynchronous

The question is what the leader waits for before telling the client the write succeeded.

ModeClient hears “ok” whenWhat it costs
Synchronousthe leader and the follower both have itOne slow follower stalls every write
Semi-synchronousthe leader and one chosen follower have itSlower writes; two copies always current
Asynchronousthe leader alone has itRecent writes can vanish on failover
In practice “synchronous” nearly always means semi-synchronous: one follower is kept in step and the rest lag behind. If that follower slows down, another is promoted to take its place.

Asynchronous replication

Writes are fast, and the leader keeps accepting them even if every follower has fallen behind. 1The price is durability: if the leader fails before a follower has received a write, promoting that follower discards the write, even though the client was told it had succeeded. This is the default in PostgreSQL and MySQL, and it is the usual choice when followers are far away.

# postgresql.conf: wait for the first standby that answers
synchronous_standby_names = 'FIRST 1 (replica_a, replica_b)'
synchronous_commit = on

Failover

When the leader dies, a follower has to take over. Three things happen, and each can go wrong:

  • Deciding the leader is dead. Usually a timeout, often 30 seconds. Too short and a busy leader is declared dead for nothing.
  • Choosing the new leader. The follower with the most recent log position loses the least.
  • Telling everyone. Clients must send writes to the new leader, and the old one must stand down when it returns. If both believe they lead, that is split brain.

Replication lag

A read from a lagging follower can miss a write the same user made a moment ago. Reading your own writes from the leader for a short while after writing avoids the most confusing case.

TutorAnswering from this note
If the client was told “ok”, how can the write still be lost after a failover?

Because “ok” from an asynchronous leader only means the leader itself has the write1. The followers receive it afterwards.

If the leader dies in that gap, the follower that takes over never saw the write. When the old leader returns it has to throw away what the new leader does not have, so the two agree again2.

Your table already holds the fix: keep one follower synchronous, and accept that each write waits for it3.
