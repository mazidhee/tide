Why does the gradient through a sigmoid shrink towards zero when its input is far from zero?

Its slope is σ(z)(1 − σ(z)). When z is far from zero, σ is nearly 0 or nearly 1, so one of the two factors is nearly 0 and almost nothing flows back.
Right. On Thursday you said the gradient “explodes”; it does the opposite. Tide will ask again in 4 days.
TideStep 3, gradients by the chain rule

That answer is the whole reason today’s network uses tanh in the hidden layer and keeps the sigmoid only at the output. Here is the network once more, with two inputs, four hidden units and one output:

h=tanh(W(1)x+b(1)),y^=σ(w(2)⊤h+b(2))(1)

For one example with label y∈{0,1} the loss is the cross-entropy:

L=−[ylny^+(1−y)ln(1−y^)](2)

Backpropagation asks one question at each layer: how much does the loss change if this layer’s input changes? Call that quantity δ. At the output, the sigmoid and the cross-entropy cancel neatly, which is why they are used together1:

δ(2)=∂L∂z(2)=y^−y(3)

To reach the hidden layer, send that error back along the same weights it came forward on, then multiply by the slope of tanh at each unit. Since tanh′(z)=1−tanh2(z), the slope is already known from the forward pass2:

δ(1)=(w(2)δ(2))⊙(1−h2)(4)

The gradients for the weights are then each layer’s δ times whatever fed into that layer:

∂L∂w(2)=δ(2)h,∂L∂W(1)=δ(1)x⊤(5)

Notice what (4) says about a hidden unit whose output is close to ±1: the factor 1−h2 is close to zero, so that unit stops learning. It is the same saturation you just described, one layer down.
